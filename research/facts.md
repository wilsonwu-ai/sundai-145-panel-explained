## T2 Manufacturing, not design, is the bottleneck (calibration, TSMC lock-in, talent)

**What the panel said:**

- [01:07] Semiconductor firms split into designers and manufacturers with a 'huge wall' between them (NVIDIA designs, TSMC makes), and the manufacturing side relies on physics-simulating tools that are slow to calibrate. (quote: "NVIDIA doesn't produce their own chips,")
- [01:47] ASML EUV machines cost about 400 million each and can take years to calibrate (machines bought 1.5 to 2 years ago only now working), which creates lock-in to existing processes. (quote: "so they sell their machines for 400 million per machine")
- [12:20] TSMC cannot afford experiments: they run locked-in recipes at high demand, and halting production 5 to 10 percent of the time would cost hundreds of millions of dollars, so smaller fabs are the place to try new paradigms. Reliability (not just making it work once) is what researchers underestimate. (quote: "hundreds of millions of dollars for them just to try it out")

- [40:00] Amdahl's Law: always attack the longest step. Today AI-driven design just optimizes for available nodes (TSMC, Intel); true optimum is co-design of process and chip but it is too slow and expensive for now, so fixing manufacturing comes first. (quote: "you should always focus on the thing which takes the longest time to optimize")
- [48:39] A second bottleneck is talent: mask sets cost millions each, chip-design talent is scarce and experienced (startup average age 35+), and OpenAI reportedly did only spec and RTL while outsourcing physical design. (quote: "there is a huge lack of talent")

## T3 Custom/hardwired silicon, the memory wall and new compute paradigms

**What the panel said:**

- [08:26] First solutions for fast custom chips may come from open PDKs rather than TSMC, because foundries require NDAs and run rigid tape-out schedules (example given: a closing date of October 1, 2026). (quote: "is going to come from all these open PDKs")
- [09:18] Startups are trying to hardwire the transformer architecture or even specific weights into chips (heard as 'Talas'); open question whether speed via TSMC or efficiency via slower specialist hardwiring wins. (quote: "where you're trying to bake the architecture either of the transformer itself")
- [13:02] Hot take: in five to ten years OpenAI will sell hardware (wearables, robotics, edge) running specific models; guess of 'easily more than 2x' speedup for a model-specific chip vs general GPU, no official numbers. (quote: "My hot take is that in maybe five to ten years, OpenAI is going to provide chips")
- [15:40] Cited example of the OpenAI chip (heard as 'Jalapeno'): a model that runs about 120 tokens/s on the fastest GPU reportedly runs about 700 tokens/s on the custom chip, roughly 7x in that setup. (quote: "So at least in this setup, it's 7x")
- [16:20] The memory wall: compute is constrained by moving data, so DRAM/HBM makers' stocks rose; wafer-scale SRAM approaches (heard as Cerebras) are faster but cost more energy per token. A small adder uses picojoules, while reading a DRAM bit costs roughly a thousand times more, which is the gain from baking things into silicon. (quote: "from a DRAM cell, that's like a thousand times more")
- [45:35] Because tape-out is expensive and slow, an intermediate path is adaptable hardware such as FPGAs for agentic workflows; panelist also bets on optical/neuromorphic compute once the design loop closes, citing the fruit-fly simulation needing a huge data center. (quote: "adaptable architectures like FPGAs")

**ELI5 analogies offered by research:** Memory wall: the chef (the compute chip) can chop vegetables in a blink, but the fridge (DRAM) is down the street, so most of the time and energy goes to walking back and forth, not cooking.
